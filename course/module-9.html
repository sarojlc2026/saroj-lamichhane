Generative AI in Course Design

Restructuring assignments so AI use requires thinking rather than replacing it.

Why this matters

Prohibition is unenforceable and permission without structure produces submissions that measure nothing. There is a third option that also happens to be better teaching.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Explain why detection and prohibition fail
  • Restructure an assignment so the graded artifact is engagement with output
  • Map AI-interaction tasks to Bloom's Taxonomy levels
  • Assess submissions with an appropriate rubric

The guide

This module sits in an accessibility course for a reason, which comes at the end.

The situation is familiar. A student asks a model to balance an equation, explain a concept, or write a paragraph. The output is pasted in. You grade work that tells you nothing about what the student understands.

Institutions have mostly gone one of two ways and both are failing.

Prohibition with detection. It is unenforceable, the detection tools are unreliable, and the false positives fall hardest on students writing in a second language. It also makes you an adversary of your own class, which is a bad position to teach from.

Permission with no structure. Produces submissions that measure nothing and teaches students that the tool does the thinking.

The third option is to change what is being assessed.

If the graded artifact is the answer, a model can produce it. If the graded artifact is the student's engagement with an answer, finding its error, adapting it to a new constraint, judging it against criteria, the model cannot produce that, because doing so requires holding the subject in your head.

So the model becomes the object of study rather than the source of the submission.

In practice, an assignment built this way asks for four things. The prompts the student used, including the ones that failed. The output exactly as returned. Their analysis, the specific cognitive task you set. And their corrected or extended work with reasoning shown. The marks sit on the third and fourth items.

Bloom's Taxonomy gives you the structure for the third item. At the Understand level, the student explains the output in their own words. At Apply, they adapt it to changed conditions. At Analyze, they locate and diagnose an error. At Evaluate, they judge two outputs against criteria. At Create, they design the prompt sequence itself and document what failed first.

The upper three levels do most of the work, because a student cannot outsource finding the flaw without understanding the material well enough to know where flaws live.

One detail that matters more than it looks. Tell students explicitly that if the model happened to be correct and they could not find an error, they should say so and explain how they verified it. Without that line, students invent errors to satisfy the assignment.

The most consistent report from instructors using this is not a change in scores. It is a change in how students talk about the material. They start arguing with the model instead of trusting it, which is the disposition you wanted all along.

Now, why this is in an accessibility course. Two reasons.

First, practical: require students to submit model output as text rather than screenshots. Screenshots of AI conversations are images of text, unreadable by assistive technology and unsearchable by you.

Second, and more fundamental: the Department of Justice, when it extended the accessibility compliance deadlines, found that generative AI cannot reliably automate the remediation of course materials at scale, and that human oversight is required. The same principle runs through both halves of this course. These tools are useful as an assistant and unreliable as a substitute, for your students doing chemistry, and for you making your course accessible.

Step by step

  1. Choose one existing assignment to convert
  2. Decide which Bloom's level the task should target
  3. Rewrite it to require prompts, raw output, analysis, and corrected work
  4. State explicitly that AI use is permitted and expected
  5. Add the clause allowing students to report that output was correct
  6. Build a rubric weighting analysis and corrected work
  7. Require text submissions, not screenshots

Checklist

  • One assignment converted and tested
  • Bloom's level is explicit
  • Prompts and raw output are required
  • Rubric weights engagement, not the answer
  • Students may report correct output honestly
  • Submissions are text, not images

Practice task

Take one assignment you already use. Rewrite it at the Analyze level: have students prompt a model until it produces something wrong, then diagnose the error.
