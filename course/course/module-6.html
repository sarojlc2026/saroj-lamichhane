Video, Captions, and Transcripts

Why automatic captions are not enough, and how to fix them efficiently.

Why this matters

Automatic captioning has improved enormously and is still not sufficient, because it fails hardest on exactly the words that carry meaning in your discipline.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • State the difference between captions, subtitles, and transcripts
  • Correct automatic captions efficiently
  • Decide when audio description is needed
  • Produce a usable transcript

The guide

Start with the distinction, because the words get used loosely.

Captions are time-synchronised text of the speech and the meaningful non-speech audio, displayed over the video. Subtitles usually mean translation and assume you can hear. Transcripts are the full text as a separate document, not synchronised.

For a lecture recording you want captions. A transcript as well is better, because it can be searched, skimmed and read at the student's own pace, and it helps far more students than it was designed for.

Now, automatic captions. Every major platform generates them, and they have become genuinely good on conversational speech. They are still not good enough, and the reason is specific: they fail hardest on specialist vocabulary. In a nursing course, dyspnea becomes this apnea. In chemistry, moles becomes molds. In statistics, heteroscedasticity becomes almost anything.

Those are the words carrying the content. A student reading uncorrected captions is not getting a slightly rough version of your lecture. They are getting a version in which the technical terms are wrong, and they have no way to know which ones.

So the workflow is: generate automatic captions, then correct them. Do not write captions from scratch; that is slow and unnecessary.

Correcting is faster than people expect. In Canvas Studio, YouTube Studio, Panopto or Kaltura, open the caption editor and play the video at reduced speed while reading along. You are looking specifically for your discipline's vocabulary, names, numbers and units. Ordinary words are usually fine.

For a fifty-minute lecture, budget twenty to thirty minutes the first time and less as you learn where your particular platform fails. If you record a series, the same terms will be wrong every time, and many editors support find and replace.

Two more things captions must carry. Speaker identification when more than one person speaks, and meaningful non-speech sound. If something audible matters, an alarm, a reaction fizzing, a sound you are asking students to identify, it belongs in square brackets in the captions.

Transcripts. Most platforms will export the corrected captions as a text file. Clean up the timestamps, add speaker labels and paragraph breaks, and post it alongside the video. This takes a couple of minutes and produces something many students use in preference to the video.

Audio description is the one people ask about least and it matters in demonstrations. If your video shows something happening that you do not describe aloud, a colour change, a technique, a step performed silently, a blind student misses it entirely. The best fix is free and requires no software: narrate what you are doing as you do it. Say now the solution turns pink rather than saying watch this. Get into that habit while recording and you will rarely need separate audio description.

Finally, do not rely on a video as the only route to content. Provide the slides and the transcript too. Multiple routes to the same content is the whole idea.

Step by step

  1. Generate automatic captions on your platform
  2. Open the caption editor and play at reduced speed, reading along
  3. Correct specialist vocabulary, names, numbers and units first
  4. Add speaker identification where more than one person speaks
  5. Add meaningful non-speech audio in square brackets
  6. Export the corrected captions as a transcript and tidy it
  7. Post the transcript alongside the video
  8. When recording new video, narrate what you are demonstrating

Checklist

  • All course video has corrected captions
  • Specialist vocabulary has been checked specifically
  • Speakers are identified where relevant
  • A transcript is posted with each video
  • New recordings narrate visual actions

Practice task

Open the automatic captions on one lecture and search for your five most important technical terms. Count how many are wrong. That number is what your students have been reading.
