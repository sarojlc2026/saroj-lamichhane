Auditing Your Own Course

A repeatable method for finding what is wrong, prioritising it, and proving progress.

Why this matters

Automated scanners return hundreds of undifferentiated findings and most remediation projects die at that point. What makes an audit finish is a sequence, not a list.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Audit a course systematically rather than reactively
  • Classify findings by type and severity
  • Prioritise remediation so students benefit soonest
  • Record before-and-after state to show progress

The guide

This module is about method, and the method matters more than any individual fix.

This pattern recurs. An institution buys a scanning tool. The tool reports four thousand issues across the course catalogue. Everyone looks at the number, nobody knows where to start, and the project quietly dies by March. The tool was not wrong. It just produced a list, and a list is not a plan.

So the first principle is: classify before you count. Sort every finding into a small number of failure types. Untagged or scanned PDFs. Images without meaningful alternative text. Video without corrected captions. Missing or incorrect heading structure. Tables without headers. Colour-only meaning. Vague link text. Keyboard traps.

Seven or eight categories will cover almost everything you find, and once findings are in categories you can see the shape of the problem. It is usually not four thousand separate problems. It is often one instructor who scans everything, or one template propagating the same error across thirty pages.

Second principle: severity is about the student, not the standard. Rank each finding by what it does to a student who needs it.

Blocking means the student cannot access the content at all. A scanned PDF. An uncaptioned video in a course with a deaf student. Fix these first, always.

Impairing means the student can access the content but with substantial difficulty. No headings in a long document. Poor alt text on an informative diagram.

Minor means a technical failure with limited practical effect. A decorative image missing an empty alt attribute.

Third principle: sequence by student impact, not by convenience. Work in this order. Assessments and anything with a safety implication. Then materials students need in the next two weeks. Then core instructional content. Then archive material. Within each tier, blocking issues before impairing.

Fourth principle: record the before state. This is the step that is easiest to skip and the one you will most regret skipping. Before you fix anything, note for each course how many findings there are in each category. When you have remediated, count again. Without a before, you cannot show anyone that anything improved, not your dean, not your accreditor, and not yourself when you need the motivation.

A simple spreadsheet is enough. Course code, failure type, count before, count after, date. That is the entire instrument.

Fifth, be honest about what one person can do. If you have thirty courses and one of you, you cannot remediate everything before the deadline. What you can do is remediate the blocking issues everywhere, then work down. A course with no blocking issues is usable by every student even if it still has minor findings.

And the durable fix is not remediation at all. It is that the next course is built accessible from the start, which is what modules one through seven were for. Remediation is triage. Training is treatment.

Step by step

  1. List every course, module or document in scope
  2. Scan each with the available automated checker
  3. Classify every finding into failure types
  4. Assign severity: blocking, impairing, or minor
  5. Record the before state per course and per category
  6. Sequence work: assessments and safety first, then near-term materials
  7. Remediate blocking issues everywhere before starting on impairing ones
  8. Recount after remediation and record the after state

Checklist

  • Scope is defined and listed
  • Findings are classified by type
  • Every finding has a severity rating
  • Before state is recorded
  • Work is sequenced by student impact
  • After state is recorded and comparable

Practice task

Take one course. Classify its findings into the eight categories and mark severity. You will almost certainly discover the problem is smaller and more repetitive than it first appeared.
