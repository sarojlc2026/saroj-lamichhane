Digital Accessibility for Faculty

Four video modules teaching you to build accessible Word documents, PowerPoint slides and PDFs. These three formats carry the large majority of the instructional material faculty produce, and they are where most accessibility failures actually live. About half an hour of video, with captions, transcripts, quick guides, practice files and knowledge checks. No login, no cost.

Start Module 1 Download everything

How long this takes. Four video modules, about 27 minutes of video in total. Allow roughly two hours to watch them, read the guides and take the knowledge checks. Allow about four hours if you do the Apply It activities on your own files, which is where the learning actually happens and is the part worth protecting when time is short. The six companion guides add roughly twenty minutes altogether.

Short on time? If you have twenty minutes, watch Module 1 and read the best-practices sheet. If you have an hour, do Modules 1 and 2 and fix one real document.

Who this is for

Instructors, tenured and non-tenured faculty, adjuncts, graduate teaching assistants, and anyone else who builds and maintains their own courses in a learning management system such as Canvas, D2L Brightspace, Blackboard or Moodle. Instructional designers and faculty developers who need something to hand people are welcome to take it and use it.

You teach, and you build your own materials. Nobody has shown you how to make them work for a student using a screen reader, and there is now a federal deadline. This course assumes no technical background and no budget, and every module ends with an activity on your own files.

It works equally at high school and university level, and it is not only about online courses. The document practices taught here apply to anything you hand a student: a printed lab manual, a slide deck projected in a lecture hall, a PDF emailed before class. Federal accessibility obligations attach to the material, not to the delivery mode.

Why this is worth your time, beyond compliance

Compliance is the deadline. It is not the reason.

Roughly one student in four has a disability affecting access to course content, and most do not disclose it, so accommodation letters systematically undercount who is affected. The same structural work also helps the student reading on a phone between shifts, the student whose first language is not English using a translation tool, and the student searching a document the night before an exam. Heading structure is what makes a reading navigable rather than a wall. Real captions are used far more by students in noisy environments than by students who are deaf.

The practical effects show up as engagement and persistence rather than as compliance scores: material students can actually get into is material they are more likely to complete. An accessible course is usually a better organised course, because the structure assistive technology needs and the structure a confused student needs are the same structure.

What you will be able to do

  • Set up Word documents, PowerPoint slides and PDFs so assistive technology can read them
  • Apply heading structure, alternative text, table headers and reading order correctly
  • Run the built-in accessibility checkers, and recognise the failures they miss
  • Judge whether a file you have been handed is usable, and fix it if not

How to use this

Work through Modules 1 to 4 in order; each builds on the last and together they take under an hour of video. Then use the companion guides for whatever your discipline throws at you, scientific notation, captions, LMS structure.

Every video has a full transcript. If you would rather read than watch, you lose nothing.

A suggested path

  1. Watch Module 1 for the principles and vocabulary. Everything else assumes it.
  2. Pick the format you use mostWord, PowerPoint or PDF, and do that module next.
  3. Do the Apply It activity on one of your own files, not a sample. The activity only works if the file matters to you.
  4. Take the knowledge check to confirm it stuck.
  5. Come back for the other formats when you next build in them.

After the course

Fix your materials in this order: assessments and anything with a safety implication first, because a student who cannot read a quiz question or a lab step is harmed immediately. Then whatever students need in the next fortnight. Then core content. Then archives.

The auditing guide covers doing this across a whole course systematically, including how to record before-and-after state so you can show what improved.

Why this course exists when free accessibility training already does

Good free accessibility training exists, and the best of it is worth taking. The W3C's own Digital Accessibility Foundations (opens in a new tab) course, produced with UNESCO and delivered through edX, is free to audit, runs 16 to 20 hours, and is genuinely excellent.

It is also, by its own description, about the web. Its stated audience is developers, designers, content authors and project managers, and its syllabus covers web accessibility principles, standards, and checking web pages. That is what it sets out to do, and it does it well.

What it does not cover, because it is not trying to, is the work most faculty actually face: a Word document, a PowerPoint deck and a PDF, uploaded to a learning management system.

That gap matters more than it sounds. Those three formats carry the large majority of instructional material in higher education, and they are where the failures that genuinely lock students out concentrate: the scanned reading with no text layer, the deck whose slides carry no titles, the untagged handout, the equation pasted in as a picture. An instructor who completes an excellent web accessibility course still does not know how to mark a header row in Word or correct reading order in Acrobat.

Free, practical, faculty-facing training on those three formats specifically is scarce. Vendor documentation covers features rather than teaching practice. Institutional training is usually behind a login and tied to one campus's systems, which is exactly why it cannot be shared. This course is the document-side complement to the web-side training that already exists, built to be worked through by an instructor with no technical background, no budget and no instructional design office to call, and published openly so that being at a well-resourced institution is not a prerequisite.

Where this course comes from

This is the open version of a training programme built and taught at Texas A&M University-Texarkana, produced with Technology Innovation and Digital Education (TIDE). The institutional version runs in Canvas, where 136 faculty across STEM, education, humanities and nursing are enrolled and 121 hours of instruction have been logged.

The method behind it came out of a systematic accessibility audit of 34 courses across four disciplinary areas, and the course was then taught to 136 faculty at Texas A&M University-Texarkana before being published here. See the case studies for how both were carried out.
