Accessible and AI-Enhanced Course Design

I build open, faculty-executable systems that let universities bring their own digital courses into compliance with federal accessibility standards, and integrate generative AI into coursework in a way that requires student thinking rather than replacing it.

The problem

Every public college and university in the United States must bring its digital course materials to WCAG 2.1 Level AA — by April 26, 2027 for entities serving populations of 50,000 or more, and April 26, 2028 for smaller entities and special district governments.

Most institutions will not make it with the staff they have. When the Department of Justice extended those deadlines in April 2026, it said why: covered entities lacked sufficient staff, and available technology could not close the gap. The Department found that current technology, including generative AI, cannot reliably automate the remediation of STEM materials at scale, and human oversight is required to ensure accessibility.

Commercial remediation is priced per document and recurs every term. The institutions furthest from compliance are the least able to buy it.

The approach

I work on the assumption that compliance has to be produced by the people who build courses, not purchased repeatedly from vendors. That means four things, all of them open and free to use:

  • An audit protocol that turns WCAG 2.1 AA into a course-by-course remediation sequence an institution can run with its own staff
  • A faculty training curriculum that transfers remediation capability to instructors
  • The Bloom's-AI Integration Model, a human-in-the-loop protocol for coursework
  • STEM remediation templates for the document types automation handles worst — chemical structures, mathematical notation, database schemas, anatomical diagrams

Where this has been used

These materials have been adopted by faculty at institutions I have never worked for, in Texas, Kentucky, New York, and Pennsylvania — including a computer science professor at the University of North Texas who found the frameworks through a conference presentation and applied them to 140 of his own students.
