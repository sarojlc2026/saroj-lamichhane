The problem

Every public college, university and school district in the United States must bring its digital course materials to WCAG 2.1 Level AA, the W3C standard adopted as the technical benchmark in the Department of Justice's Title II web and mobile accessibility rule. The deadlines are April 26, 2027 for entities serving populations of 50,000 or more, and April 26, 2028 for smaller entities and special district governments.

Most institutions will not make it with the staff they have. When the Department extended those deadlines in April 2026, it said why: covered entities lacked sufficient staff, and available technology could not close the gap. The Department found that current technology, including generative AI, cannot reliably automate the remediation of STEM materials at scale, and that human oversight is required to ensure accessibility.

Commercial remediation is priced per document and recurs every term. The institutions furthest from compliance are the least able to buy it, and nothing they buy accumulates: an institution in its third year of paying a vendor is no more capable than it was in its first.

Compliance has to be produced by the people who build courses, not purchased repeatedly from vendors.

The approach

That principle produces four things, all of them open and free to use, and each published here in full. They share one design idea: intervene before a barrier is created rather than after somebody finds it.

An audit protocol

Turns WCAG 2.1 Level AA into a course-by-course remediation sequence an institution can run with its own staff.

Why it helps. Automated checkers return thousands of undifferentiated errors, which tells a dean nothing and tells a faculty member only that they have failed. This classifies every finding by consequence to students and records who can actually fix it, so remediation starts with the locked midterm rather than with a missing document title, and so the work goes to the person able to do it.

A faculty training curriculum

Transfers remediation capability to instructors, so compliance is produced by the people building courses. Published in full as a free self-paced course covering Word, PowerPoint and PDF, the three formats faculty actually build in. Excellent free training already exists for web accessibility, including the W3C Foundations course. Equivalent open training for the document formats is scarce, and that is the gap this fills.

Why it works where other training does not. It is organised by the file types faculty already make, not by the success criteria of the standard, so nobody has to learn a compliance vocabulary before fixing their first document. Every module ends on a real file of the participant's own rather than a sample, because nobody transfers a skill they only practised on someone else's work. And nothing in it depends on a particular institution's LMS, branding or policy, which is why it can be published openly and used anywhere.

The Bloom's-AI Integration Model

A human-in-the-loop protocol for course design, applied to how assignments are built rather than to how students are policed.

Why it matters now. The same Department of Justice finding that drove the deadline extension, that automated tools cannot be relied on without human oversight, is the argument this model makes about coursework: the value is in the human judgment applied to machine output, not in the output. So the model changes what is assessed. If the graded artifact is the answer, a model can produce it. If it is the student's engagement with an answer, finding its error, adapting it to a new constraint, judging it against criteria, the model cannot, because that requires holding the subject in your head.

STEM remediation templates

For the document types automation handles worst: chemical structures, mathematical notation, database schemas, anatomical diagrams.

Why faculty need them. General accessibility guidance was written for photographs and simple charts, and applied to a structural formula it produces "diagram of a benzene ring", which is accurate and useless. These give the notation routes and description templates for the material that general guidance leaves an instructor to invent alone, including how to describe a diagram without handing students the answer it is meant to assess.

What comes next

All four operate at the point where courses are built or checked. There is a stage before that, and it is where the decisions actually get made.

Where accessibility currently enters. Institutions offering distance education across state lines must adhere to the Council of Regional Accrediting Commissions' Interregional Guidelines for the Evaluation of Distance Education, the instrument behind reciprocity agreements covering some 2,200 institutions across 49 states. Across nine hallmarks of quality, including one devoted entirely to curricula, the guidelines do not mention accessibility, disability or assistive technology. They were last updated in 2011, seven years before WCAG 2.1 existed. The proposed 2021 replacement mentions accessibility twice in twenty-three guidelines, once listing it among student support services alongside tutoring and coaching.

So accessibility is not ignored. It arrives late, after programs are approved and courses are built, which is why the work is done repeatedly and by the institutions least able to afford it. A program-level framework, addressing accessibility during academic program design rather than after it, is in development and will be published here alongside the others.

What is published here

Everything described above is on this site, at no cost and with no login. The course is the training curriculum in full, with video, captions, transcripts, practice files and knowledge checks. The resources are the frameworks as readable pages, with Word and tagged PDF versions for anyone who wants to print or adapt them. The case studies describe four pieces of work in enough detail to judge the method.

These are working documents rather than finished publications. They are revised as the underlying research develops, as the regulatory picture changes, and whenever someone using them reports a problem. Version numbers are on each one, and corrections are credited. If something here is wrong or unclear, contact Saroj Lamichhane.

Accessibility of this site. Built and tested to WCAG 2.1 Level AA. If you meet a barrier here, please report it and it will be fixed.
