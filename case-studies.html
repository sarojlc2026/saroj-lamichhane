Case Studies

Four pieces of work, described in enough detail to judge the method rather than the claim. Each sets out the problem, the work carried out, what changed, and what would be done differently.

1. Auditing 34 courses against WCAG 2.1 Level AA

Texas A&M University-Texarkana, 2025 to 2026

The problem

An institution facing the Department of Justice Title II deadline needs to know two things: how bad is it, and what gets fixed first. Most accessibility reports answer neither. They return a list of several thousand undifferentiated errors, which tells a provost nothing actionable and tells a faculty member only that they have failed.

The work

The audit covered 34 distinct post-secondary courses spanning STEM (BIOL 466, CHEM 1305, EE 340), health and nursing (NURS 332, NURS 417, NURS 541), business (MGT 395, SCM 310, ACCT 430, MKT 445), and humanities and social sciences (WGSS 1301, HIST 1302, PSCI 2305).

Rather than counting errors, failures were classified by type and by consequence:

  • Blocking. Content a student using assistive technology cannot reach at all. Image-only untagged PDFs, static embedded slide decks that hide their own text, video with no captions or transcript.
  • Degrading. Content that is reachable but much harder to use. Missing heading structure, tables built for layout, link text that says nothing.
  • Cosmetic. Real failures that do not stop anyone. Contrast slightly under threshold, missing document title.

The classification is the whole point. It converts a flat error list into a sequence, so remediation can start with assessments and safety-critical laboratory procedures instead of starting alphabetically.

What changed

The output was a course-by-course remediation roadmap rather than a flat report: every finding logged, classified by consequence, and assigned a place in the sequence. The audit also produced the failure-type taxonomy that the training course and the published audit protocol are both built on.

What would be done differently

The audit ran before any faculty training. Reversing that order would have been better. Faculty who have taken the training can prepare their own course before it is reviewed, which makes the audit shorter and, more importantly, makes it feel like support rather than inspection. Accessibility work fails politically far more often than it fails technically.

2. Building a faculty training course that transfers the capability

Digital Accessibility Training, Texas A&M University-Texarkana

The problem

Remediation bought from a vendor is priced per document and recurs every term. Nothing accumulates. The institution is no more capable in year three than it was in year one, and the courses that need help most sit at institutions least able to pay.

The alternative is to train the people who build the courses. That runs into a well-known wall: accessibility is a technical subject with its own vocabulary, and faculty encountering it cold tend to disengage quickly.

The work

The course was designed and taught by Saroj Lamichhane, lead instructor, with Elizabeth Murphy, Instructional Designer III, with 136 faculty enrolled across STEM, education, humanities and nursing, and 121 hours of logged instruction.

Three design decisions did most of the work:

  • Organised by file type, not by standard. Faculty do not arrive asking about Success Criterion 1.3.1. They arrive with a Word document. Each module takes one artefact they already make and shows how to make it accessible, with the standard introduced as the reason rather than the frame.
  • Every module ends on a real file. The Apply It activity is done on the participant's own course material, not a sample. Nobody transfers a skill they only practised on someone else's document.
  • Nothing institution-specific. No component depends on a particular LMS configuration, on institutional branding, or on A&M System policy. It is assembled from WCAG, the Quality Matters rubric, and ordinary faculty development practice, which is why it can be published openly and used anywhere.

What changed

Each trained instructor becomes a point of compliance capability applied to every course they build afterwards. That is the difference between additive and multiplicative work, and it is the reason enrollment rather than completion is the reported figure: the course is a capability being distributed, not a certificate being issued.

The course is now published at no cost, with video, captions, transcripts, practice files and knowledge checks. Take it here.

What would be done differently

The Acrobat module depends on software many participants do not have, which was underestimated. Acrobat Pro is licensed at some institutions and not at others, and a module a participant cannot practise is a module they do not retain. The published version names the free alternatives up front, and it should have been built that way from the start.

3. Redesigning a first-year course across 22 sections

UNIV 1100 University Foundation, Texas A&M University-Texarkana

The problem

University Foundation is a first-year student success course. Moving it to fully online asynchronous delivery across 22 sections puts accessibility and retention in the same decision: the students most affected by an inaccessible course are disproportionately the students the course exists to retain.

The work

Converted all 22 sections to asynchronous online delivery holding to WCAG 2.1 Level AA throughout, rather than remediating afterwards. Structural work included consistent module architecture across sections, semantic heading structure in every page and document, captioned media with transcripts, and a mentor-led support framework running alongside the content.

Designing to the standard from the start rather than retrofitting is not merely cheaper. Retrofitting produces a course that is technically compliant and structurally incoherent, because accessible structure and good course structure are largely the same thing.

What changed

Of 171 students surveyed, 81.3 percent confirmed that the redesign and its mentor-led support framework enhanced their academic success.

What would be done differently

The survey measured student perception, which is worth having but is not a learning outcome. Pairing it with persistence and completion data across sections would be better. That comparison is what would actually establish whether accessible design affects retention, and it is the question now being pursued in doctoral research.

4. Resolving a conflict between accessibility and assessment integrity

Faculty consultation, Biology, April to May 2026

The problem

A Lecturer of Biology teaching a hybrid laboratory course raised an objection that most accessibility guidance does not address. Her worksheets required students to identify anatomical structures from diagrams. Descriptive alternative text on those diagrams would name the structures, handing the answer to precisely the students the accommodation exists to support.

She was right. This is a real conflict, not a misunderstanding, and telling her to add alt text anyway would have been wrong.

The work

The assessment was redesigned rather than either side compromised:

  • Uploaded the original high-resolution slide decks to the LMS, so students could enlarge diagrams without loss
  • Supplied a structured three-slides-per-page PDF for note-taking
  • Developed text-based description exercises that evaluate the same conceptual knowledge without depending on visual identification
  • Enrolled her in the training course, so the next conflict could be resolved without outside help

The general principle, now published in the alternative text guide, is this: describe what a sighted student sees, not what they are asked to conclude. A sighted student sees lines, structures and arrangement. They do not see a label naming the answer. Alternative text should give the same raw information and no more. Where the visual task genuinely cannot survive translation to text, the accessible route is an alternative assessment of the same underlying knowledge, not a degraded description.

What changed

One course, which is a small result. It matters because it is the objection that stops faculty cooperating, and answering it convincingly does more for institutional compliance than another checklist. The last step, enrolling her rather than solving it for her, is the part worth keeping.

What would be done differently

This should have been written up immediately. It was reconstructed later from correspondence, and details were lost. Faculty consultations are where the method is actually tested, and they are now documented as they happen.
