Open Resources

Four frameworks, each published as a page you can read straight through, with a Word version if you would rather print one or adapt it for your own institution. No login, no cost, no institutional agreement.

If you are a faculty member trying to make your own course accessible and you have no instructional design office to call, these were written for you. Licensed CC BY 4.0 (opens in a new tab).

Reading these on the web is the accessible route. A web page reflows on a phone, scales to 200 percent without blurring, and works with every screen reader without depending on how your PDF viewer handles tags. The Word version is offered as an alternative for printing and adaptation, not as the primary format. That is the same argument these documents make about your course materials, so it would be strange to do otherwise here.

Multi-Course Accessibility Audit Protocol

Version 1.1 · About 14 minutes to read

A survey method for auditing course shells against WCAG 2.1 Level AA that produces a prioritised remediation sequence rather than an undifferentiated error list. Findings are classified by consequence, blocking, degrading or cosmetic, and logged with an estimate of who can actually fix each one, which is the field most audits omit and the one that determines whether anything happens.

Written up from the working instrument used across an audit of 34 courses spanning STEM, health and nursing, business, and humanities.

STEM Document Remediation Protocol

Version 1.0 · About 12 minutes to read

Most instructors paste equations in as pictures, because that is what the software makes easy. A screen reader announces nothing, the image blurs when enlarged, and it will not reflow on the phone most students actually read on. This sets out four practical routes to accessible notation, the Word equation editor, LaTeX, the LMS editor, and remediating what you already have, with plain-text conventions for fallbacks and a section on laboratory and procedural documents where a student who cannot read a safety step is not merely inconvenienced.

Scientific Diagram Alternative Text Guide

Version 1.0 · About 12 minutes to read

Published alternative text guidance was written for photographs. Applied to a structural formula it produces "diagram of a benzene ring", which is accurate and useless. This guide gives templates by diagram type, single molecules, reaction schemes, curly-arrow mechanisms, apparatus and titration curves, and addresses a problem most guidance ignores entirely: how to describe a diagram without handing the answer to the students an accommodation exists to support.

The Bloom's-AI Integration Model

Version 1.0 · About 11 minutes to read

Institutions are splitting into prohibition on one side and unstructured permission on the other, and both are failing. This model does a third thing: it changes what is assessed. If the graded artifact is the answer, a model can produce it. If the graded artifact is the student's engagement with an answer, finding its error, adapting it to a new constraint, judging it against criteria, the model cannot, because that requires holding the subject in your head.

Includes a mapping of each Bloom's level to an AI-interaction task, a one-page assignment template, worked chemistry examples at the Analyze and Apply levels, and a rubric.

Developed from A Holistic Framework for AI-Enhanced, Accessible Online Course Design, presented at OLC Innovate 2026 following peer review.

In development: accessibility at the program-design stage

The four frameworks above all operate once a course exists or is being built. There is an earlier stage, and it is where accessibility is currently absent.

Institutions offering distance education across state lines adhere to the Council of Regional Accrediting Commissions' Interregional Guidelines for the Evaluation of Distance Education (opens in a new tab) as a condition of reciprocity, covering roughly 2,200 institutions across 49 states and more than three million students studying exclusively at a distance. Across nine hallmarks and some seventy criteria, including a hallmark devoted to curricula, the guidelines do not mention accessibility, disability or assistive technology. They were last updated in 2011. The proposed 2021 successor (opens in a new tab) mentions accessibility twice in twenty-three guidelines, once as a student support service listed with advising and tutoring.

The effect reaches state policy. When Texas revised its Principles of Good Practice (opens in a new tab) in 2023, its one curriculum-level accessibility principle could be traced only to a voluntary quality rubric, because the interregional guidelines contained nothing to trace it to.

A program-level accessibility framework is being developed to address this: a way for institutions to settle accessibility once, during program design and approval, rather than repeatedly during course remediation. It will be published here under the same licence as the others when it is ready.

Nothing is being claimed for it yet. It is listed here because the gap it addresses is documented and worth knowing about whether or not this particular framework turns out to be the answer.

Accessible document templates

Start from one of these and the accessibility work is already done. Heading structure, document title, language and reading order are set correctly, so you inherit the structure rather than repairing it later. Replace the content and keep the styles.

  • Syllabus template (DOCX)
  • Lecture slide template (PPTX)
  • Course review workbook (XLSX)

Use the built-in heading styles rather than formatting text to look like headings, and the file stays accessible as you edit it. Modules 2 and 3 of the course explain why, and how to do the same to a document you already have.
